I still remember walking into the **College of Engineering, Pune (COEP)** back in 2014. As a fresh Computer Engineering undergrad, I’ll be honest: I barely knew how to handle a computer. My journey started from scratch, fueled by nothing but an eager curiosity to understand this "magic box."

Fast forward to 2026, and the landscape has shifted entirely. My alma mater is now **COEP Technological University**, and the "magic" isn't just in the box—it’s in the Intelligence (AI) running on it. But as I watch the new generation of engineers, I can't help but wonder: **Are we trading our foundational "grit" for "glance-and-go" solutions?**

### The "Old School" Struggle (2014–2018)
In my day, there was no LLM to explain a segmentation fault. If we had a C++ or Java assignment, we lived in the lab.

* **The Documentation Dive:** We didn't prompt; we read. We poured over thick language docs to understand how a Pointer worked or why a JVM behaved a certain way.
* **The Team Brainstorm:** Projects weren't generated in seconds. We spent hours in the lab, scribbling on whiteboards, arguing over logic, and manually stitching modules together.
* **The "Weeks" of Work:** A single complex assignment took weeks. But those weeks were where the learning happened. We didn't just "get the code done"; we built a mental map of how every "small piece" fit into the whole.

### The AI Dilemma: Efficiency vs. Expertise
Today’s undergrads have a 24/7 tutor in their pocket. While this is a superpower, it’s also a trap. 

The risk is that students might "cheat on themselves." If AI provides the answer in 2 seconds, you miss the 2 hours of **"productive struggle"** that actually hardwires the basics into your brain. When you don't struggle with the syntax, you might never truly master the logic.

### How to Use AI Without Losing Your "Engineering Soul"
So, how can current students stay sharp? Here are a few points I’d add for the next generation:

1.  **The "First Principles" Rule:** Never use AI for a concept you haven't tried to sketch out on paper first. If you're learning Data Structures, try to implement a Linked List manually before asking Copilot to do it.
2.  **Use AI as a "Reviewer," not a "Creator":** Write your code first. Then, ask AI: *"How can I optimize this?"* or *"What edge cases did I miss?"* This turns AI into a mentor rather than a ghostwriter.
3.  **Prompt for "Why," not just "What":** Instead of asking for the code, ask: *"Explain the logic behind the Red-Black Tree balancing act."* If the AI gives you code, force yourself to add comments to every line to prove you understand it.
4.  **Embrace the "Lab Culture":** Don't let AI replace the team brainstorm. Sit with your peers, argue about the architecture, and use AI only when the human collective gets stuck. The "soft skills" of engineering—collaboration and debate—cannot be automated.

### Final Thoughts
AI is a tool, just like the compilers and IDEs were for us in 2014. It can make you a **"10x Engineer,"** but only if you have the **"1x" foundations** solid.

To the engineers and beyond: The AI can write the code, but you must be the one who knows if that code is right, ethical, and efficient. **Don't skip the struggle—it’s where the engineer is born.**
